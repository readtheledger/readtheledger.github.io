How the calculation works

For a Canadian resident in the simple case, subtract the income-year threshold, multiply a positive difference by 15%, then cap the estimate at actual gross OAS received in that income year. Zero OAS gives zero repayment. This does not calculate GIS, EI repayment, non-resident rules or the adjustments in CRA’s worksheet.

Annual repayment and withholding differ. Repayment is reconciled on the return for that income year, with tax already withheld credited as tax deducted. That return can also set estimated withholding from July of the following year to June of the year after. Dividing the annual estimate by 12 here illustrates withholding; it is not a second tax or a guaranteed deduction.

Published full-recovery ceilings are reference values for a maximum pension, not your personal cap. Service Canada marks 2026 ceilings as estimates through September. Partial-year, deferred and age-75 pensions can differ; use actual records and the official worksheet.

A worked example

$100,000 of 2025 income exceeds $93,454 by $6,546. At 15%, repayment is $981.90, assuming actual gross OAS of at least that amount. With $8,000 OAS, the cap does not bind; $81.83 per month illustrates later withholding. With only $500 gross OAS, the annual estimate is capped at $500. Zero OAS gives zero repayment.

Check your assessment

Use CRA’s line 23500 worksheet for your return and Service Canada’s advisory letter for withholding. If expected income falls substantially, CRA describes reduced withholding through T1213(OAS). A withholding change does not itself remove annual repayment.

Frequently asked

Which threshold?
$90,997 for 2024 income, $93,454 for 2025, $95,323 for 2026.
Does 2026 income only matter in July 2027?
No. It can create repayment on the 2026 return and affect later withholding. Withheld tax is credited on the return.
Is the monthly result exact?
No. The annual estimate divided by 12 is an illustration. Your assessed return, pension and adjustments govern.
